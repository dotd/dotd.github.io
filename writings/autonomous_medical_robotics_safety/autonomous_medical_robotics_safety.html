Where We Are on Autonomous Medical-Robotics Safety

A short survey of where the field actually stands on safety as surgical robots climb from teleoperation toward autonomy — what has been defined, what has been demonstrated, and what is still missing.

By Dotan Di Castro

Autonomy is arriving in the operating room the way it arrived everywhere else — unevenly, one narrow task at a time, and faster than the frameworks meant to keep it safe. A surgical robot today is still, overwhelmingly, an instrument held at arm’s length: the surgeon moves, the machine follows, and the intelligence in the loop is human. But the trajectory is unmistakable. Perception is getting good enough to see tissue the way a surgeon does; control is getting precise enough to execute sub-millimeter motions the human hand cannot; and learned models are beginning to propose — and, in narrow settings, to perform — steps of a procedure on their own. The question this essay asks is not whether autonomy is coming. It is whether our notion of safety is keeping pace with it, and where, honestly, we stand right now.

The first thing a maturing field does is try to name its own stages, and medical robotics is no exception. The now-standard vocabulary comes from Yang and colleagues, who in 2017 proposed a six-level scale of surgical autonomy in Science Robotics, deliberately echoing the levels-of-autonomy ladder that the automotive world had adopted for self-driving cars. The levels run from Level 0, no autonomy — pure teleoperation, the master–slave systems that dominate the market today — up through Level 1 robot assistance (the machine steadies or constrains, the human still drives), Level 2 task autonomy (the robot performs a discrete, surgeon-initiated task like suturing a defined stretch), Level 3 conditional autonomy (the system plans and the surgeon approves and supervises), Level 4 high autonomy (the robot makes decisions under a surgeon’s oversight), and finally Level 5, full autonomy — a robot that could, in principle, perform an entire operation with no human in the loop. The value of the scale is less in any single rung than in what it makes visible: safety is not one problem but a moving target that changes character at every level, and the burden of assurance shifts from the surgeon toward the machine as you climb.

How the physical-world fields verify safety

Before asking what surgical robotics should do, it helps to see what the more mature physical-AI fields — automotive above all, but also aviation, robotics, and control theory — have actually converged on. No single technique is sufficient; the field layers them. Seven families recur across the literature:

  1. Scenario-based testing. Enumerate the situations inside a defined operational design domain and argue coverage rather than raw mileage (PEGASUS).
  2. Simulation and X-in-the-loop. Billions of virtual miles, digital twins, and SIL/HIL/VIL testing that reach where road testing cannot.
  3. Falsification and adversarial search. Actively search for inputs that break the system rather than run a fixed suite (Breach, S-TaLiRo, VerifAI).
  4. Formal methods. Prove behavioral guarantees — RSS, reachability analysis, control barrier functions, neural-network verification (ACAS Xu).
  5. Runtime assurance. Wrap the learned component in a verified monitor that takes over on leaving a safe envelope (Simplex doer–checker, shielded RL).
  6. Statistical and probabilistic validation. The “how many miles?” argument, accelerated rare-event evaluation, and uncertainty/OOD detection for the ML stack.
  7. Assurance cases and standards. The structured argument that ties evidence together — GSN, ISO 26262, ISO 21448/SOTIF, UL 4600.

The through-line is a single admission: for an open-world AI system you cannot test your way to safety, so assurance becomes a stack — coverage plus search plus proof plus runtime guards plus a statistical and argumentative case. SOTIF and UL 4600 exist precisely because the older fault-based model has nothing to say about the failure that matters most here — the perception was simply wrong.

The chapters below take the seven in turn. Each sketches the idea and its main tools, then asks the question that matters for this essay: how much of it transfers to the operating room, and where does the transfer break? For orientation, the most autonomous surgical demonstration to date — the Smart Tissue Autonomous Robot (STAR, which in 2022 performed laparoscopic intestinal anastomosis in live pigs, covered by IEEE Spectrum) — still ran the majority of the procedure under close human supervision. That is roughly Level 2–3, and it is the frontier.

Approach 01 of 07

Scenario-based testing

The oldest and most industrial idea: you cannot drive every road, so you describe the world as a structured set of scenarios and argue you have covered them. Menzel, Bagschik and Maurer formalized the now-standard three-layer split — functional, logical, and concrete scenarios — while the whole space is bounded by an Operational Design Domain, itself now a standardized object in ISO 34503. Scenarios are exchanged in formats like ASAM OpenSCENARIO and OpenDRIVE; Germany’s PEGASUS and SET Level projects, the open CommonRoad benchmark, NHTSA’s testable-cases work, and Waymo’s public safety framework are the reference implementations; Riedmaier et al. survey the field.

Coverage vs. gap. Scenario methods are strong on the known long tail and helpless on the unknown one: you can only test situations you thought to enumerate. For surgery the ODD analogue barely exists — there is no agreed taxonomy of “surgical scenarios,” no OpenSCENARIO for anatomy, and anatomical variation is far higher-dimensional than a highway. This is the most borrowable idea in the list and the least developed.

Approach 02 of 07

Simulation and X-in-the-loop

Because real miles are scarce and dangerous, most testing happens in simulators — CARLA (Dosovitskiy et al.), NVIDIA DRIVE Sim, LGSVL, Microsoft AirSim (repo), with background traffic from SUMO — and along the X-in-the-loop ladder: model-, software-, hardware-, and vehicle-in-the-loop, each trading fidelity for cost. Waymo synthesizes journeys at scale in Simulation City; scenario languages like Scenic and platforms like MetaDrive generate the inputs; a dedicated survey catalogs the high-fidelity landscape. The perennial enemy is the sim-to-real gap, attacked with domain randomization.

Coverage vs. gap. Simulating deformable tissue, bleeding, and tool–tissue interaction is far less mature than rigid-body vehicle dynamics: the physics is harder and the ground truth scarcer. There is, in effect, no CARLA for the abdomen — which caps how much of the assurance argument surgery can move in silico.

Approach 03 of 07

Falsification and adversarial search

Rather than hope a fixed suite trips a bug, search for one. Encode a requirement in Signal Temporal Logic (Maler & Ničković) whose quantitative robustness is positive when satisfied and negative when violated, then let an optimizer drive it below zero. S-TaLiRo, its Python successor PSY-TaLiRo, and Breach do exactly this; VerifAI (paper) and Scenic wrap it around learning-based autonomy. In parallel, the adversarial-examples literature — Szegedy et al., Goodfellow et al., and physical-world attacks like the graffiti’d stop sign — shows how brittle perception is, while coverage-guided tools (DeepXplore, DeepTest) hunt those failures automatically.

Coverage vs. gap. Falsification needs both a formal specification and a simulator to falsify against; surgery has neither in mature form. And the adversarial robustness of surgical perception — endoscopic video, tissue and instrument segmentation — is almost entirely unstudied compared with road-scene classifiers.

Approach 04 of 07

Formal methods

The strongest guarantee is a proof, and two flavors matter. First, proofs about behavior: Mobileye’s Responsibility-Sensitive Safety defines a formal safe-distance envelope; Hamilton–Jacobi reachability computes the set of reachable states to show collisions are impossible; control barrier functions render a safe set invariant. Second, proofs about the network: Reluplex and its successor Marabou (repo) verify input–output properties of neural nets, abstract-interpretation tools like ERAN scale it, and the annual VNN-COMP with winners like α,β-CROWN tracks the frontier — the canonical benchmark being the ACAS Xu collision-avoidance network.

Coverage vs. gap. Formal methods need a model. Vehicle kinematics have a good one; a beating heart or a retracting bowel does not. And neural-net verification is still confined to modest networks and simple properties — nowhere near a full surgical perception stack. The proofs are real; their reach is narrow.

Approach 05 of 07

Runtime assurance

If you cannot prove the whole system safe offline, guard it online. The classic pattern is Sha’s Simplex architecture: a trusted simple controller and a decision module stand ready to snatch control from the complex/AI controller the instant the state leaves a verified safe envelope — recently generalized to black-box components. Reinforcement-learning variants add formal shields and are surveyed under safe RL; runtime-verification monitors like RTAMT check temporal-logic properties live; and aviation has standardized the idea in ASTM F3269. RSS itself doubles as a runtime monitor.

Coverage vs. gap. A surgical “safety envelope” is hard to define — what is the safe set for a suture in soft tissue? Force and geometry limits exist for some tasks, but a general checker is open. Even so, this is arguably the most tractable borrowable idea for near-term Level 2–3 systems: bound the tool, not the whole surgery.

Approach 06 of 07

Statistical and probabilistic validation

At some point you must make a numerical claim, and here lies the field’s most sobering result: Kalra & Paddock’s RAND analysis showed you would need hundreds of millions to tens of billions of miles to demonstrate statistically that an AV is safer than a human — brute-force road testing alone is hopeless. The response is accelerated, rare-event evaluation via importance sampling (Huang, Zhao et al., joint distributions, deep importance sampling, corner-case generation), plus per-decision uncertainty from the ML side: conformal prediction, Bayesian and MC-dropout uncertainty, deep ensembles, and out-of-distribution detection. California’s public disengagement reports are the crude field metric.

Coverage vs. gap. The surgical event of interest — a serious complication — is rarer, more heterogeneous, and harder to simulate than a crash, and there is no naturalistic “miles” denominator. Recall that STAR, the field’s most autonomous demonstration, still ran under supervision. The statistical case for a Level 4–5 surgical robot is, today, nowhere near closed.

Approach 07 of 07

Assurance cases and standards

Finally, the argument that binds all the evidence. A safety case — usually written in Goal Structuring Notation (GSN) — is an explicit, auditable claim–argument–evidence tree that a system is acceptably safe. Automotive layered ISO 26262 (faults) with ISO 21448 / SOTIF (performance limits with no fault — the AI-specific hazard), and UL 4600 (ULSE) wraps the whole autonomous product in a goal-based safety case; Koopman & Wagner’s critique is the standard reading, and aviation’s DO-178C is the older cousin. Medicine already has IEC 62304 (device software), ISO 13485 (quality), and IEC 60601 — and, crucially, IEC 60601-4-1, the first standard to address a medical device’s degree of autonomy — plus the FDA’s evolving AI/ML SaMD framework.

Coverage vs. gap. Medicine has strong process standards but nothing yet like SOTIF or UL 4600’s goal-based safety case for an autonomous agent. The regulatory question — how do you certify a robot that decides? — is, for surgery, essentially open. Which returns us to where the Yang scale began: the levels were easy to name; the assurance that lets us climb them is the hard, unfinished work.
